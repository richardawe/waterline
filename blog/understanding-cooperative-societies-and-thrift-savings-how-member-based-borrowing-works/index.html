Understanding Cooperative Societies and Thrift Savings: How Member-Based Borrowing Works

Cooperative societies and thrift savings groups are member-owned financial arrangements where individuals pool their money to provide loans and savings opportunities for one another. Unlike traditional banks, these groups operate on the principle of mutual aid, allowing members to access credit based on their contributions and the trust of the group. This model is particularly vital for SMEs and individuals who may lack the formal collateral required by commercial lenders.

How Cooperative Societies Work

A cooperative society is a formal organization where members voluntarily join together to achieve a common economic goal. In the context of finance, these are often "credit unions" or "savings and loan cooperatives."

Members contribute a regular amount of money—often monthly—into a central fund. This pool of capital serves as the source for loans. Because the members are the owners of the cooperative, the primary goal is not to maximize profit for external shareholders, but to provide affordable financial services to the members themselves.

The Mechanics of Member-Based Borrowing

Borrowing within a cooperative or thrift group differs significantly from applying for a bank loan. The process generally follows these structural patterns:

Contribution-Based Credit

In many cooperatives, a member's ability to borrow is tied to their savings. For example, a member might be allowed to borrow a multiple of what they have contributed to the pool. This ensures that the loan is backed by the member's own equity in the society.

Peer Guarantee and Trust

In smaller thrift groups, often called "ajo" or "esusu" in certain regions, trust is the primary collateral. Members vouch for one another. If a member wishes to take a loan from the collective pot, the group may approve it based on the borrower's reputation and history of consistent contributions.

Interest Distribution

While cooperatives charge interest on loans to ensure the fund grows and remains sustainable, this interest does not go to a corporate entity. Instead, it is typically redistributed among members as dividends at the end of the year or used to improve the society's services.

Thrift Savings Groups vs. Formal Cooperatives

While the terms are often used interchangeably, there are structural differences between informal thrift groups and formal cooperative societies.

Thrift Groups (Informal): These are often small, tight-knit circles of friends, colleagues, or traders. They may operate on a rotational basis, where one member receives the entire pool of contributions each month until everyone has had a turn. These are primarily savings vehicles rather than long-term lending institutions.

Cooperative Societies (Formal): These are usually registered legal entities with a governing board and a written constitution. They can offer more complex financial products, such as long-term loans for land or equipment, and may partner with other financial institutions to expand their reach.

The Role of Development Finance

For SMEs that outgrow the capacity of a local cooperative, other forms of institutional support exist to bridge the gap. For instance, the Bank of Industry (BOI) serves as Nigeria's oldest and largest development finance institution. The BOI provides long-term, low-interest financing to SMEs and industrial projects, and these funds are often channelled through participating commercial and microfinance banks to reach the end-user.

While a cooperative provides immediate, community-based liquidity, development finance institutions like the BOI focus on larger-scale industrialization and long-term growth, showing the different layers of credit available to a growing business.

Advantages and Risks of Member-Based Finance

The Benefits

  • Accessibility: There are fewer bureaucratic hurdles and less stringent documentation than in commercial banks.
  • Lower Costs: Interest rates are often more favorable because the goal is mutual support rather than profit.
  • Financial Discipline: The requirement for regular contributions encourages a culture of saving.

The Risks

  • Liquidity Constraints: If too many members borrow at once, the society may run out of available cash.
  • Default Risk: In informal groups, if a member disappears or cannot pay, the loss is shared by the other members.
  • Governance Issues: Without professional management, formal cooperatives can suffer from poor record-keeping or mismanagement of funds.

Summary of the Lending Cycle

To visualize how this works, consider the cycle: Members contribute $\rightarrow$ A common pool is created $\rightarrow$ A member applies for a loan $\rightarrow$ The group approves based on savings or trust $\rightarrow$ The member repays with interest $\rightarrow$ The interest increases the pool or is paid back to members as dividends.

FAQ

What is the main difference between a cooperative and a commercial bank?

A cooperative is owned and managed by its members for their mutual benefit, whereas a commercial bank is a profit-driven institution owned by shareholders.

How are loan repayments typically handled in thrift groups?

Repayments are usually made on a set schedule, with interest flowing back into the common pool to benefit all members of the group.

Can SMEs use cooperatives for business growth?

Yes, many small business owners use cooperatives to access working capital that might be harder to obtain from traditional lenders due to strict collateral requirements.

How Payday Loans and Salary Advances Are Priced and Repaid

Payday loans and salary advances are short-term credit products that are designed to cover gaps until the borrower's next income. Their pricing is determined by interest rates and fees that vary among lenders, and repayment is typically due on the next payday or through a direct debit from the borrower's account. Understanding how these costs are calculated and how repayment works helps borrowers compare offers and avoid expensive rollover.

What are payday loans and salary advances?

These products are often used by individuals who need a small amount of money for a short period, such as until the next salary payment. They can be offered by traditional microfinance institutions, digital lending platforms, or employer‑based salary advance schemes. The key feature is that the loan is expected to be repaid in a single installment that coincides with the borrower's regular income cycle. They are often marketed as a quick solution for unexpected expenses such as medical bills or utility payments. Because the amount borrowed is usually small, the application process can be simple, sometimes requiring only identification and proof of income.

How short-term credit is priced

Lenders set the cost of short‑term credit by combining an interest charge with additional fees. The interest is usually calculated as a percentage of the principal, and when this percentage is annualized it can produce a high annual percentage rate (APR). Some lenders use a flat fee per unit of currency borrowed, while others apply a daily or weekly rate. The exact structure varies by lender and by the type of product, and the total cost can be influenced by the length of the loan and any processing charges. In addition to interest, lenders may charge origination fees, service fees, or document fees, which can increase the total cost. When comparing offers, looking at the annual percentage rate (APR) can help standardize the cost over a full year, even though the loan term is much shorter.

How repayment works

Repayment is generally scheduled for the date when the borrower receives their next salary or other regular income. Many lenders require the borrower to provide a post‑dated cheque or to authorize an automatic debit from their bank account. If the due date passes without full repayment, lenders may impose a late fee or allow a rollover, which extends the loan for an additional period, often with an extra charge. Some employer‑linked salary advances are deducted directly from the next paycheck, which can simplify the process. If the borrower is unable to repay on time, some lenders offer a rollover option that extends the due date for an additional fee, while others may report the default to credit bureaus. It is important to understand the consequences of missing a payment before taking the loan.

Regulatory environment in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the regulatory landscape for short‑term credit includes oversight by the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) and the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC). The FCCPC has taken action against digital lenders that shamed or harassed borrowers, including contacting people in a borrower's phone contacts, treating such practices as unfair to consumers. In 2022, the FCCPC together with the CBN issued guidelines requiring digital money lenders to register and be approved before operating, following widespread complaints about predatory loan apps. These rules remain in force, creating a framework that aims to protect consumers from abusive practices. The CBN also oversees microfinance banks, which are required to follow prudential guidelines, and the FCCPC's guidelines aim to ensure that digital lenders are transparent about their terms and do not engage in unfair collection practices.

Alternative sources of short‑term credit

Microfinance banks (MFBs), which are licensed and supervised by the CBN under a tiered structure (Unit, State, National), provide an alternative source of short‑term credit for individuals and micro‑small businesses that commercial banks often find too costly to underwrite. Because MFBs operate under regulatory oversight, their loan products may offer more transparent pricing and repayment terms compared with some unregistered digital lenders. In addition to MFBs, some employers offer salary advance programs that allow employees to access a portion of their earned wages before the regular pay date, often with little or no interest.

Risks and considerations

Borrowers should be aware that short‑term credit often carries a high cost when expressed as an annual rate. Rollover or late fees can increase the total amount owed, and repeated use of such loans can lead to a cycle of debt. The risk is greater when lenders are not transparent about fees or when they use aggressive collection practices, including contacting the borrower's contacts, which the FCCPC has deemed unfair. Borrowers should carefully read the loan agreement to identify all fees and the exact repayment schedule. Using short‑term credit repeatedly can create a reliance that makes it difficult to build savings.

The short answer

Nigeria has three private credit bureaus licensed by the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN): CRC Credit Bureau, FirstCentral Credit Bureau, and CreditRegistry. Lenders report borrowers' repayment data to these bureaus, which compile individual and business credit reports that other lenders use when deciding whether to approve a loan, a credit card, or an overdraft.

Why credit bureaus exist in Nigeria

Before credit bureaus became common, Nigerian lenders had to make loan decisions almost blindly. Each bank only knew what a borrower had done with that specific bank, and there was no easy way to spot someone running up debt across several lenders at once.

The CBN's solution was to license private credit bureaus and require regulated lenders to share repayment data with them. That created a shared database: a borrower who defaults at one bank becomes visible to the next bank they approach. The bureaus sit at the centre of this data-sharing system, and they are the reason your borrowing history in Nigeria can follow you from one lender to another.

The three CBN-licensed credit bureaus

Although the bureaus compete with each other for lender clients, they all perform the same core job: collecting loan data and producing credit reports. Here is what each one does within the system.

CRC Credit Bureau

CRC Credit Bureau is one of the earliest private bureaus licensed by the CBN. It collects repayment data from a wide range of Nigerian lenders, including commercial banks, microfinance banks, and other regulated institutions, and assembles that data into credit reports on both individuals and businesses.

FirstCentral Credit Bureau

FirstCentral Credit Bureau, sometimes referred to simply as FirstCentral, works with Nigerian banks and other lenders to gather and store credit information. Like the other bureaus, it produces credit reports that subscribing lenders can pull during underwriting.

CreditRegistry

CreditRegistry is the third CBN-licensed bureau. It focuses on collecting loan and repayment data from Nigerian financial institutions and turning that information into credit reports. Together with CRC and FirstCentral, it forms the third leg of Nigeria's licensed credit-reporting infrastructure.

How the system actually works, step by step

The flow of information in Nigeria's credit-bureau system is fairly straightforward once you see it laid out.

  1. You apply for a loan, overdraft, or credit facility from a regulated lender. The lender runs a credit check, which means it requests your credit report from one or more of the licensed bureaus.
  2. The bureau pulls together your file using data it has already received from other lenders and supplies the report back to the new lender.
  3. If the loan is approved, the lender starts reporting your repayment behaviour to the bureaus: each payment made on time, each payment missed, and any changes to your outstanding balance.
  4. Over time, your file grows into a record of how you have handled credit across the lenders you have used.
  5. The next time you apply for credit somewhere else, that updated history is what the new lender sees.

This is why your behaviour on one loan, whether it is a small microfinance facility or a large commercial overdraft, can shape your ability to borrow elsewhere.

What goes into a Nigerian credit report

A credit report is essentially a summary of your borrowing and repayment history. Based on the data lenders send to the bureaus, a typical report includes:

  • The loans and credit facilities you currently hold or have held recently
  • The lenders you have borrowed from
  • Your repayment pattern, including any missed payments, late payments, or written-off debts
  • Your outstanding balances and the limits on your credit facilities
  • Public information relevant to creditworthiness, such as insolvency records where applicable

Lenders use this picture to judge how reliably you have handled debt in the past, which is a strong predictor of how you are likely to handle a new loan.

What this means in practice for borrowers

The credit-bureau system has practical consequences that show up every time you try to borrow.

Your repayment history is the biggest factor

Because the bureaus exist to record how you repay, the single most important thing on your file is your track record. Consistent on-time payments build a positive history over time. Missed payments, defaults, and outstanding judgements show up clearly and can take a long time to fade from view.

New lenders see your full borrowing picture

When you apply for a new loan, the lender can see your existing facilities and how you are handling them. Taking on more debt than you can comfortably service, even if you technically qualify on income, can count against you because the bureau data shows the total picture.

Older history can still matter

Closed accounts and older loans remain part of your file for some time, which means a default from years ago can still surface in a new lender's checks. This is one reason borrowers who have had credit problems in the past are often advised to focus on rebuilding good behaviour on whatever new credit they can access.

How the bureaus differ, and why it rarely matters to borrowers

The three bureaus are separate companies, each with its own database and its own client list of lenders. In theory, your file at CRC might not be identical to your file at FirstCentral or CreditRegistry, because not every lender reports to every bureau.

In practice, though, the big banks and major lenders usually report to more than one bureau, and most underwriting systems check across the network. For a borrower, the main effect of having three bureaus is competition in the market, which can help keep service standards and data quality higher than they would be in a single-bureau system.

Common questions about Nigeria's credit bureaus

Are the bureaus regulated?

Yes. The three bureaus listed above are licensed and supervised by the Central Bank of Nigeria, which sets the rules for how they operate and how lenders share data with them.

Does every lender in Nigeria report to the bureaus?

Regulated lenders that grant consumer or commercial credit are part of the reporting framework. If you borrow from a licensed bank, microfinance bank, or similar institution, your repayment behaviour is very likely to be reported.

What if I find an error on my file?

If you believe your credit report contains information that is wrong or out of date, the standard approach is to raise the issue with the bureau and with the lender that supplied the data. The bureau will investigate and amend the file where the information is found to be inaccurate.

How long does information stay on a credit report?

The bureaus retain credit information so that lenders can see a meaningful history of how a borrower has handled debt. The exact duration depends on the type of record and the bureau's policies, and borrowers who want specifics should check directly with the bureau in question.

Why was I refused a loan even though I earn a good salary?

Income is only one part of a lender's decision. Your credit report shows how you have actually behaved with debt in the past, how much you already owe, and whether you have missed payments. A strong salary combined with a poor repayment history, or with too many existing commitments, can still lead to a refusal.

FAQ

How many credit bureaus operate in Nigeria?

Three private credit bureaus are currently licensed by the Central Bank of Nigeria: CRC Credit Bureau, FirstCentral Credit Bureau, and CreditRegistry. They collect, store and share credit information on individuals and businesses.

What information do Nigerian credit bureaus collect?

Lenders send repayment data to the bureaus, including details of loans, credit facilities, repayment performance, and outstanding balances. The bureaus compile this into individual and business credit reports that other lenders can request when assessing a new loan application.

Do all Nigerian lenders report to the credit bureaus?

Banks and most regulated lenders that grant consumer or commercial credit are part of the reporting system. Borrowers should assume that any formal loan or credit facility they hold is likely visible to other lenders through the bureau network.

Why do credit bureaus matter for borrowers in Nigeria?

Your bureau file is what most lenders check before approving a new loan, credit card, or overdraft. A clean repayment history can speed up approvals and improve terms, while missed payments or defaults can make new borrowing harder or more expensive.

Can errors in a credit report be corrected?

Borrowers who spot incorrect information on their file should raise a dispute with the bureau and the lender that supplied the data. The bureau will investigate and update the record if the information is found to be inaccurate.

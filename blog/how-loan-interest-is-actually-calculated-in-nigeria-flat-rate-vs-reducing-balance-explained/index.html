How Loan Interest Is Actually Calculated in Nigeria: Flat Rate vs Reducing Balance Explained

The short answer

Loan interest in Nigeria is usually calculated in one of two ways: a flat rate, where interest is charged on the original loan amount for the whole term, or a reducing balance (also called declining balance), where interest is charged only on the portion of the principal still outstanding. The same headline percentage can therefore mean very different things in naira terms, and understanding the difference is the single most useful skill for comparing any loan offer.

How a flat-rate loan is calculated

With a flat rate, the lender takes the quoted interest rate, multiplies it by the full principal, and divides that figure by the number of repayment periods. The result is a fixed interest charge that does not change as you repay.

Imagine a ₦100,000 loan at a flat rate of 12% per year over 12 months:

  • Total interest = 12% × ₦100,000 = ₦12,000
  • Total repayable = ₦112,000
  • Monthly repayment = ₦112,000 ÷ 12 = about ₦9,333

The lender earns ₦12,000 even though the outstanding balance drops every month as the borrower pays back part of the principal. Flat rates are popular with consumer lenders, microfinance institutions and buy-now-pay-later providers because they are easy to advertise and easy for borrowers to understand at a glance.

The trade-off is that you pay interest on money you have already given back, which makes flat-rate loans more expensive than the headline number suggests.

How a reducing-balance loan is calculated

A reducing-balance (or "diminishing balance") loan charges interest on whatever you still owe at the start of each period. As your outstanding principal falls with every repayment, the interest portion of your next instalment falls too.

Using the same ₦100,000, 12% per year, 12-month example, monthly interest is roughly 1% of the previous month's balance. Early repayments are interest-heavy, but by the final months almost the entire instalment goes to clearing the principal.

Total interest on a reducing-balance loan at 12% over a year is meaningfully lower than the ₦12,000 charged under the flat method — the gap widens as loan terms get longer. This is why a 12% reducing-balance loan and a 12% flat-rate loan are not the same product.

Flat vs reducing balance: a side-by-side example

Two borrowers each take ₦500,000 over 24 months at a quoted 15% rate. Borrower A's lender uses flat interest, Borrower B's uses reducing balance:

  • Flat (15%): Total interest ≈ 15% × ₦500,000 × 2 = ₦150,000. Total repayable = ₦650,000. Monthly repayment ≈ ₦27,083.
  • Reducing balance (15%): Total interest is noticeably lower because the average outstanding principal is roughly half of the original amount. Approximate total repayable falls into the high ₦500,000s.

The same quoted rate, a very different real cost.

Why the difference matters in Nigeria

In Nigeria, lenders range from commercial banks and microfinance banks to fintech lenders, cooperative societies and informal "esusu" or "ajo" groups. Each may quote rates in different ways, and some are required to disclose an Annual Percentage Rate (APR) that captures fees and compounding, while others only state a flat monthly or annual figure.

This matters most for small-ticket, short-tenure borrowing. A customer finance loan from a retailer, a salary advance from a fintech app or a small business loan from a microfinance bank is often priced on a flat monthly basis, which can translate into an effective APR several times higher than the headline number. Larger commercial bank term loans and most mortgage-style facilities tend to use reducing-balance or amortising structures, which makes them easier to compare across providers.

Microfinance banks in Nigeria are licensed and supervised by the Central Bank of Nigeria under a tiered structure (Unit, State and National) and primarily serve individuals and micro/small businesses that commercial banks often find too costly to underwrite. That role makes it especially important for MFB customers to read the schedule carefully, because MFB pricing is more likely to be expressed as a flat or simple-interest monthly figure rather than a fully amortised reducing-balance rate.

Other factors that change the real cost of a loan

The interest calculation method is only one piece of the puzzle. A few other items worth checking on any offer:

  • Fees and charges: arrangement fees, processing fees, insurance, stamp duty and mandatory account opening can each add 1–3% to the cost of the loan.
  • Repayment frequency: monthly, weekly or daily schedules change how quickly the principal is paid down and therefore how much reducing-balance interest accrues.
  • Compounding: some lenders add unpaid interest back into the balance each period. Daily compounding on a flat-style facility is one of the most expensive structures in the market.
  • Early repayment: does the lender charge a penalty for settling the loan ahead of schedule? Flat-rate products in particular can feel punitive because the lender has already "earned" interest on principal that will not be outstanding for the full term.
  • Mandatory savings or deposits: some lenders require a percentage of the loan to be held in a savings account that you can only access at maturity, effectively raising the real rate.

How to compare two loan offers fairly

When two lenders give you quotes, do not compare the headline percentages directly. Instead:

  1. Ask each lender which method they use (flat or reducing balance).
  2. Ask for the total amount repayable in naira over the full term, including all fees.
  3. Ask for an amortisation schedule showing principal, interest and balance after each repayment.
  4. Convert both offers to an APR if the lender provides one, or use an online amortisation calculator to estimate it.
  5. Run a quick mental check: if the total interest is close to (rate × principal × years), it is flat; if it is much lower, it is reducing balance.

Key takeaways

  • Flat rate = interest on the original principal for the whole term. Simple to read, more expensive in practice.
  • Reducing balance = interest only on what is still owed. Cheaper for the same quoted rate.
  • A 15% flat loan is not the same as a 15% reducing-balance loan; always compare in naira, not in percentages.
  • Always ask for a full amortisation schedule and a breakdown of all fees before signing.

Frequently asked questions

Which interest method is cheaper for borrowers? For the same quoted rate, the reducing balance method is usually cheaper because interest is charged only on the outstanding principal. A flat rate looks simpler but costs more in total because the full principal is used for the calculation throughout the loan's life.

Can the same loan quote mean different things at different lenders? Yes. Two lenders can advertise the same percentage rate and the same monthly repayment, but one may be using flat-rate interest while the other uses reducing balance. Comparing the total cost in naira, and the APR where disclosed, is the only reliable way to tell which is actually more expensive.

Do Nigerian microfinance banks use flat or reducing balance rates? Microfinance banks in Nigeria, which are licensed and supervised by the Central Bank of Nigeria under a tiered Unit, State and National structure, serve borrowers that commercial banks often consider too costly to underwrite. Like other lenders, MFBs vary in how they compute interest, so borrowers should always ask which method is being used and request a full amortisation schedule before signing.

How can I quickly tell which method a lender is using? Ask the lender for the total interest payable over the full loan term. If that figure is close to (rate × principal × years), it is a flat-rate loan. If it is noticeably lower, the lender is almost certainly using a reducing balance or declining-balance method.

How to Collect Unpaid Estate Service Charges Fairly: A Guide for Residents' Associations and Facility Managers

Residents' associations and facility managers often face the challenge of unpaid estate service charges. Recovering these amounts is essential for maintaining common areas, security, and amenities, but it must be done in a way that respects residents' rights and complies with Nigerian law. The following sections outline a fair, step‑by‑step approach that balances the need for payment with obligations under the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023 and consumer protection rules enforced by the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC).

Understanding the Legal Framework

Before initiating any collection activity, it is important to know the legal boundaries that apply.

  • Data protection: The NDPA 2023 applies to any organisation that processes personal data in Nigeria, including estates that keep records of who owes them. Processing must have a lawful basis (such as consent, contractual necessity, or a legitimate interest), be limited to what is necessary, and include appropriate security safeguards. The Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC) oversees compliance.
  • Consumer protection: The FCCPC has taken action against lenders that shame or harass borrowers, including contacting third parties or publishing defaulters’ lists. Such conduct is considered unfair and can lead to sanctions.

Understanding these rules helps you design a collection process that is both effective and lawful.

Setting Up Transparent Billing and Communication

Clarity from the outset reduces misunderstandings and makes later follow‑up easier.

  • Clear service charge agreements: Ensure that every resident has a written agreement that outlines the amount, frequency, due date, and any permissible late fees or interest. The agreement should also specify how notices will be delivered (e‑mail, posted notice, hand‑delivery).
  • Regular statements: Send periodic statements that show the opening balance, charges incurred, payments received, and the closing balance. A simple, itemised format helps residents verify what they owe.
  • Accessible payment options: Offer multiple ways to pay (bank transfer, online platforms, designated collection points) and publish clear instructions. When residents see that paying is straightforward, they are more likely to stay current.

Engaging Residents Early and Fairly

Early, respectful contact often resolves arrears before they grow.

  • First reminder: When a payment is missed, send a polite reminder within a reasonable time frame. State the amount due, the original due date, and how to pay. Avoid language that implies blame or threatens disclosure.
  • Second reminder: If the first reminder yields no response, send a follow‑up that references the earlier notice and repeats the payment details. Keep the tone helpful, perhaps asking if the resident is facing any difficulties that could be addressed.
  • Personal contact: For persistent cases, a brief, courteous phone call or face‑to‑face conversation can uncover underlying issues (e.g., banking problems, temporary hardship) and allow you to work out a payment plan.

Throughout these steps, record the dates and content of each communication. This documentation is useful if you later need to demonstrate that you acted reasonably and in line with data protection rules.

Escalation Steps When Payments Are Overdue

If informal reminders do not produce results, a more formal process may be needed while still respecting legal limits.

  • Formal notice: Issue a notice that clearly outlines the outstanding amount, any agreed‑upon late charges (if your contract permits them), and a final deadline for payment. State that further action may be taken if the debt remains unpaid, but avoid mentioning specific legal proceedings unless you are prepared to follow through.
  • Offer a payment plan: Where residents show willingness but lack the lump sum, propose a structured instalment plan. Get the agreement in writing and ensure it complies with the original service charge terms.
  • Mediation: Many estates have a mediation clause in their governing documents. Engaging a neutral third party can help reach a mutually acceptable solution without resorting to court.

Only after these steps should you consider formal legal action, such as filing a claim in a small claims court. Even then, ensure that any claim is based on accurate records and that you have respected data protection obligations throughout the process.

Respecting Data Protection and Consumer Rights

Every interaction with resident data must meet the standards set by the NDPA 2023 and the FCCPC.

  • Lawful basis: Rely on the contractual necessity of processing payment information to manage the estate. If you rely on legitimate interest, conduct a balancing test to ensure it does not override the resident’s privacy rights.
  • Data minimisation: Collect only the information needed to pursue the debt (name, unit number, outstanding amount, payment history). Avoid gathering unrelated personal details.
  • Security: Store records in a secure system with access limited to authorised personnel. Use encryption where appropriate and regularly review access logs.
  • No harassment: Do not contact a resident’s employer, family members, or neighbours about the debt. Do not publish lists of defaulters on notice boards, social media, or any public forum. The FCCPC treats such actions as unfair and may impose penalties.
  • Right to access and correction: Residents have the right to view their personal data and request corrections. Respond promptly to any such requests.

When to Involve Formal Dispute Resolution

If all internal efforts fail, you may need to escalate to an external forum.

  • Small claims court: Suitable for relatively low‑value claims. Ensure you have the service charge agreement, payment records, and a copy of all notices sent.
  • Alternative dispute resolution (ADR): Some estates prefer arbitration or conciliation, which can be faster and less adversarial than court proceedings.

Throughout any formal process, continue to treat the resident with dignity and avoid any actions that could be construed as harassment or intimidation.

Conclusion

Collecting unpaid estate service charges is a necessary part of estate management, but it must be carried out within the bounds of Nigerian data protection and consumer protection law. By establishing transparent billing, communicating respectfully, following a graduated reminder process, and safeguarding personal data, residents' associations and facility managers can recover arrears while maintaining trust and goodwill within the community. Always document each step, keep information secure, and refrain from any practice that could be seen as shaming, harassing, or otherwise unfair. When in doubt, consult a legal professional familiar with the NDPA 2023 and FCCPC guidelines to ensure your approach remains compliant.

FAQ

What is the first step when a resident misses a service charge payment?

Start with a polite, written reminder that states the amount due, the due date, and how to pay. Keep the tone neutral and avoid any language that could be seen as threatening or shaming.

Can we share a resident's payment status with other owners or contractors?

Only share personal data when you have a lawful basis under the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023, such as the resident's consent or a legitimate interest that does not override their rights. Always limit the information to what is necessary and keep it secure.

What should we do if reminders are ignored?

Follow a graduated process: send a second reminder, then a formal notice that outlines any agreed‑upon late fees or interest (if permitted by your agreement), and finally offer a meeting to discuss any difficulties. Escalate to mediation or a small claims court only after these steps have been exhausted.

How do we avoid breaking consumer protection rules while collecting dues?

Avoid contacting third parties about the debt, using abusive language, or publishing lists of defaulters. The Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC) treats such actions as unfair, so keep all communication direct, respectful, and confidential.

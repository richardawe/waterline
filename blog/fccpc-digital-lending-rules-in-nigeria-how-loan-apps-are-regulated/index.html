Who regulates digital loan apps in Nigeria

Digital money lenders — the companies behind the small, short-term loan apps commonly downloaded on phones in Nigeria — are jointly overseen by the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC) and the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN). The FCCPC leads on consumer-protection and registration matters, while the CBN is Nigeria's banking and monetary regulator, responsible for licensing deposit money banks and microfinance banks and setting the Monetary Policy Rate (MPR). A third body, the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC), enforces data-protection rules that affect how lenders handle borrower information.

The 2022 registration framework

Under guidelines issued in 2022, the FCCPC, together with the CBN, requires digital money lenders operating in Nigeria to register and be approved before they can take on borrowers. The rules were introduced after widespread complaints about predatory practices by some loan apps, and they remain in force. The core effect of the framework is straightforward: a digital lender is not meant to be offering loans to Nigerians from a position of legal limbo. Registration is the gateway through which the regulators can bring a lender into the formal system, monitor what it does, and act if it crosses the line.

For a borrower, this matters because the registration requirement creates a clear regulator-to-lender relationship. Where there is none, there is effectively no route to complain, no licence to revoke, and no consequence for bad behaviour.

How the rules interact with banking supervision

Because the CBN already supervises deposit money banks and microfinance banks, the joint framework essentially extends that supervisory logic to app-based lenders. The CBN's role covers the broader monetary and prudential environment — interest-rate conditions, the banking licence regime and the MPR — while the FCCPC's contribution sits in the consumer-facing layer: how a lender markets itself, how it treats the people borrowing from it, and whether it can lawfully operate at all.

This split is important for readers. A digital lender is not automatically a bank. The rules recognise that digital lending is its own category of business, sitting between informal roadside lending and a fully regulated microfinance bank, and apply a tailored registration regime rather than a full banking licence.

Data protection: the NDPA angle

Digital lending apps work by collecting personal data — often extensive amounts of it, directly from a borrower's phone. The Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023 is the law that governs how lenders and loan apps may collect, process and share borrowers' personal data, and it is enforced by the NDPC. While the NDPA is a general data-protection framework and not a lending-specific law, its provisions apply fully to loan apps because those apps process borrowers' personal information in the course of their business.

In practice, this means a digital lender's handling of borrower data is a regulated activity in its own right, separate from — but overlapping with — the FCCPC/CBN lending rules. A complaint about how an app uses contacts, messages, photos or location data is a data-protection question and falls within the NDPC's remit; a complaint about unfair lending terms or harassment falls within the FCCPC's consumer-protection remit.

Where the regulators overlap

A single bad experience with a loan app can involve several regulators at once. The registration and consumer-protection angle sits with the FCCPC. If the lender is also a licensed microfinance bank or otherwise under CBN supervision, the banking side of the issue sits with the CBN. If personal data has been misused, that sits with the NDPC under the NDPA. Knowing which body handles which complaint helps direct a grievance to the right place rather than scattering it across agencies.

What borrowers can check before installing a loan app

Because the 2022 framework requires digital money lenders to be registered and approved, a first practical step for any borrower is to verify that the lender actually appears in the regulated population. Beyond that, the structural factors that tend to separate safer apps from riskier ones include transparency of terms, the data the app asks to access on the phone, the cost of the loan relative to its term, and whether the lender is identifiable by a registered Nigerian company. None of these is a substitute for reading the actual loan agreement, but together they are useful filters.

Why the rules matter for the broader market

The 2022 framework was a response to a specific problem: a fast-growing digital lending market with significant consumer harm. By making registration compulsory and putting both the FCCPC and CBN on the hook, the rules created a single entry point at which a lender can be vetted, and a clear line of accountability afterwards. Layering the NDPA on top gives borrowers a separate, enforceable set of rights over their personal data. The result is a regulatory architecture in which a loan app cannot easily operate in Nigeria as an untraceable, unregulated entity.

Key takeaways

  • Digital money lenders in Nigeria must register and be approved under the joint FCCPC/CBN guidelines first issued in 2022 and still in force.
  • The FCCPC leads on consumer protection and registration; the CBN leads on banking and monetary regulation.
  • The NDPA 2023, enforced by the NDPC, governs how lenders collect, process and share borrower data.
  • Complaints about lending conduct route to the FCCPC, data-missuse complaints to the NDPC, and bank-supervision issues to the CBN.

FAQ

Who regulates digital loan apps in Nigeria?

The Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC) and the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) jointly regulate digital money lenders, with the FCCPC handling consumer-protection and registration matters and the CBN overseeing banking and monetary policy.

Do loan apps have to register before operating?

Yes. Under the 2022 joint guidelines from the FCCPC and CBN, digital money lenders are required to register and be approved before they can operate in Nigeria.

Why were these rules introduced?

The registration framework was introduced after widespread complaints about predatory practices by some digital lending apps, and remains in force.

What does data protection law mean for loan apps?

The Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 governs how lenders and loan apps collect, process and share borrowers' personal data, and is enforced by the Nigeria Data Protection Commission.

What should a borrower look for before using a loan app?

Check whether the lender is registered and approved under the FCCPC/CBN framework, read the full loan terms before accepting, and be cautious about granting access to contacts, photos or other personal data on your phone.

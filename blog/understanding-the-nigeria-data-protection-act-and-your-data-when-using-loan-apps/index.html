Understanding the Nigeria Data Protection Act and Your Data When Using Loan Apps

The Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023 is the primary law that governs how personal data is collected, processed, stored and shared in Nigeria. For borrowers who use digital loan apps, the NDPA sets clear expectations about what lenders may do with their information and what rights borrowers retain over that data. Understanding these rules helps borrowers make informed choices and know when a lender may be overstepping its bounds.

What the NDPA Covers for Loan App Users

The NDPA applies to any entity that handles personal data, including loan apps that collect names, phone numbers, bank details, employment information and other identifiers. The law requires lenders to have a lawful basis for processing data, most commonly the borrower’s consent or the necessity of performing a contract (the loan agreement). Consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous; borrowers should be able to say no without being denied the loan unless the data is truly essential for the credit decision.

Beyond consent, the NDPA imposes principles of purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation and integrity. This means a loan app should only collect data that is needed to assess creditworthiness, should not keep it longer than necessary, must keep it accurate and must protect it against unauthorised access, loss or disclosure.

Key Rights Borrowers Have Under the NDPA

Under the NDPA, borrowers are recognised as data subjects and enjoy several enforceable rights:

  • Right to be informed: Lenders must provide a clear privacy notice explaining what data is collected, why it is collected, how it will be used, who it may be shared with and how long it will be retained.
  • Right of access: Borrowers can request confirmation of whether their data is being processed and obtain a copy of that data in a commonly used format.
  • Right to rectification: If any personal data is inaccurate or incomplete, borrowers can ask for it to be corrected.
  • Right to erasure (right to be forgotten): In certain circumstances, such as when the data is no longer needed for the original purpose or consent is withdrawn, borrowers can request deletion of their data.
  • Right to restriction of processing: Borrowers can ask that the lender limit how their data is used, for example, while a dispute about accuracy is resolved.
  • Right to data portability: Where processing is based on consent or a contract and carried out by automated means, borrowers may receive their data in a structured, machine‑readable format and transmit it to another controller.
  • Right to object: Borrowers can object to processing for direct marketing or for purposes related to the lender’s legitimate interests, unless the lender demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds.

These rights are not absolute; there are exemptions for legal obligations, public interest or the exercise of legal claims. However, for typical loan‑app operations, the rights above are the primary tools borrowers can use to control their personal information.

How Loan Apps Must Comply: Registration and Data Practices

Before a digital money lender can offer loans to the public, it must obtain approval from the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) and register with the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC). This requirement stems from joint guidelines issued in 2022 that were introduced after widespread complaints about predatory lending practices. Registration is not a one‑time step; lenders must maintain compliance with both financial regulations and data‑protection rules.

In practice, compliance means:

  • Publishing an accessible privacy policy that meets NDPA standards.
  • Obtaining explicit consent before accessing contacts, messages, photos or other sensitive phone data.
  • Implementing technical and organisational security measures such as encryption, access controls and regular security audits.
  • Keeping records of consent and data‑processing activities to demonstrate accountability.
  • Establishing procedures for handling data‑subject requests, including verification of identity and timely response (generally within one month).
  • Notifying the NDPC and affected borrowers of any data breach that is likely to result in a risk to rights and freedoms, without undue delay.

Failure to meet these obligations can result in enforcement actions by the NDPC, fines, or sanctions from the CBN and FCCPC, including suspension of the lender’s ability to operate.

Practical Steps Borrowers Can Take to Protect Their Data

While the law places duties on lenders, borrowers can also adopt habits that reduce risk:

  1. Read the privacy policy before installing or using a loan app. Look for clear statements about data collection, sharing with third parties, and retention periods.
  2. Limit permissions on your mobile device. Grant only those accesses that are strictly necessary for the loan application (for example, you may not need to give the app access to your contacts or SMS unless the lender can justify it).
  3. Use strong authentication. Create a unique password for the loan‑app account and enable two‑factor authentication if the service offers it.
  4. Monitor your accounts. Regularly check bank statements and credit reports for any unauthorised activity that could stem from a data leak.
  5. Exercise your rights. If you are unsure what data a lender holds, submit a data‑access request. If you find inaccuracies, ask for correction. If you no longer need the service, you can request deletion of your data where appropriate.
  6. Keep evidence. Save copies of any correspondence with the lender regarding data requests, complaints or consent withdrawals. This documentation can be useful if you need to escalate a matter to the NDPC or FCCPC.

By staying vigilant and exercising the rights granted by the NDPA, borrowers can better safeguard their personal information while still accessing the credit they need.

Conclusion

The Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 creates a framework that balances the need for lenders to assess creditworthiness with the borrower’s right to privacy and control over personal data. For anyone using a loan app, the law means that lenders must be transparent, obtain proper consent, keep data secure and respect specific data‑subject rights. Regulators such as the NDPC, CBN and FCCPC oversee compliance, and borrowers have practical tools — reading policies, limiting app permissions, using strong security and exercising access, correction and deletion rights — to help protect themselves. Understanding these provisions empowers borrowers to engage with digital lending services more confidently and to hold lenders accountable when they fall short of the law’s standards.

FAQ

What does the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 require of loan apps?

The NDPA 2023 requires loan apps to obtain clear consent before collecting personal data, to process data only for legitimate and disclosed purposes, to keep data secure, and to allow borrowers to access, correct, or delete their information. Lenders must also register with the appropriate regulators before operating.

Which bodies enforce the NDPA and oversee loan apps in Nigeria?

The Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC) enforces the NDPA 2023. The Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) and the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC) jointly oversee the licensing and conduct of digital money lenders, including the requirement that they be registered and approved before offering loans.

What rights do borrowers have under the NDPA regarding their loan‑app data?

Borrowers can request confirmation of whether their data is being processed, obtain a copy of that data, ask for inaccuracies to be corrected, request deletion or restriction of processing in certain cases, and object to processing for direct marketing. They also have the right to be informed about any third‑party sharing of their data.

How can borrowers verify that a loan app complies with the NDPA and regulator rules?

Borrowers should look for evidence that the lender is registered with the CBN/FCCPC (often displayed on the app or website), read the privacy policy to see what data is collected and how it is used, and check whether the app provides clear options to consent, withdraw consent, or exercise data‑subject rights. If information is missing or unclear, the lender may not be fully compliant.

What steps can borrowers take to protect their personal data when using loan apps?

Borrowers can limit the amount of personal information they share to what is strictly necessary for the loan, use strong, unique passwords for app accounts, enable two‑factor authentication if available, regularly review app permissions on their device, and keep records of any communication with the lender regarding data requests or complaints.

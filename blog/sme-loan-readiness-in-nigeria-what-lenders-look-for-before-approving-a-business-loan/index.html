What does "loan ready" actually mean for an SME in Nigeria?

Loan readiness is the state your company is in the moment you walk into a bank or open a lending app. It means your paperwork is organised, your numbers tell a clear story, and your business can credibly show a lender that the money will be used well and paid back on time. Nigerian lenders, whether commercial banks, microfinance institutions or development finance outfits, use loan readiness as a quick filter to decide which applications deserve deeper review.

In practice, most Nigerian lenders judge loan readiness across four broad buckets: documentation, cash flow, business stability, and the borrower's character. Each bucket has its own checklist, and weakness in one area can sink an otherwise strong application.

Documentation: the paperwork lenders want to see first

Documentation is usually the first gate. Before a lender even looks at your numbers, they want to confirm that your business legally exists, who is behind it, and what it is licensed to do. While requirements vary by lender and loan type, applications that move forward typically include:

  • CAC registration documents, including your Certificate of Incorporation, Form CAC 2 (Return of Allotment) and Form CAC 7 (Particulars of Directors), or the Business Name registration equivalent for a simpler structure.
  • Valid means of identification for the directors and key signatories, such as a National ID, international passport, driver's licence or voter's card, plus utility bills as proof of address.
  • Tax-related documents, including a Tax Identification Number (TIN) and evidence of recent tax filings or payments, which signal that the business is operating formally.
  • Sector-specific permits or licences, where applicable, such as NAFDAC registration for food and cosmetics, or tourism and hospitality permits for hotels and travel operators.
  • Bank statements, usually covering the most recent six to twelve months, which give the lender a window into how money actually moves through the business.
  • Financial statements, ideally prepared or reviewed by an accountant, covering at least one to two years of trading.

A common reason applications stall is not that the business is weak, but that the paperwork is incomplete or inconsistent. Lenders treat missing documents as a signal of poor record-keeping, which feeds directly into their assessment of credit risk.

Cash flow: the single most important factor

Once documentation is out of the way, lenders focus on cash flow. A business can look impressive on paper and still be declined if the numbers do not show that loan repayments can comfortably fit into the company's monthly inflows.

Lenders generally want to see:

  • Stable or growing monthly revenue over at least six to twelve months, supported by bank statements rather than self-reported figures.
  • A healthy operating cash cycle, meaning the gap between paying suppliers and receiving customer payments is short enough that working capital does not run out.
  • A debt service coverage ratio that gives the new loan room to breathe. In plain English, the business should bring in enough each month to cover its existing obligations plus the new loan repayment, with a margin left over for operations and unexpected expenses.
  • A clear loan purpose, with realistic projections for how the borrowed funds will generate the cash that repays them.

Lenders do not need perfect numbers. They need numbers that are honest, consistent and explained. If revenue dropped for a clear reason, say so. If a large customer pays late, show the pattern. Context makes a stronger case than polished figures with no story behind them.

Business stability and operating history

Most Nigerian lenders prefer businesses that have been operating for at least twelve to twenty-four months. A newer business is not automatically excluded, but it carries more uncertainty, so lenders compensate by asking for stronger collateral, a more detailed business plan, or a director with a verifiable track record in the same industry.

What lenders are really assessing under "stability" is risk concentration. They look at:

  • Customer concentration, meaning how dependent the business is on one or a few buyers. A business that loses 60 percent of its revenue when one client leaves is a riskier bet.
  • Supplier concentration, which affects whether input prices or delivery schedules are predictable.
  • Location risk, from footfall in a retail shop to exposure to a particular market or community.
  • Industry risk, where some sectors are considered more volatile than others.

Character: the often-overlooked piece

Nigerian lenders still place weight on the borrower's character. This includes the directors' credit history, which lenders can check through the Credit Bureau, as well as their industry experience and reputation. A director who has successfully run and repaid a previous business loan carries credibility. A director with a history of bounced cheques, court judgments, or unpaid loans carries a warning sign.

Character also includes how you behave during the application. Lenders pay attention to responsiveness, transparency about weaknesses in the application, and willingness to provide additional information when asked. A borrower who is evasive or argumentative signals trouble ahead.

Collateral and guarantor requirements

Many commercial banks require some form of security for SME loans, whether physical assets such as property, equipment or vehicles, or a lien on the business's receivables or stock. Microfinance institutions may accept lighter collateral such as equipment or inventory.

Where collateral is hard to produce, some borrowers look to development finance institutions for support. The Bank of Industry (BOI), for example, is Nigeria's oldest and largest development finance institution, providing long-term, low-interest financing to SMEs and industrial projects, often channelled through participating commercial and microfinance banks. Programmes like this are designed to widen access for businesses that would otherwise struggle to meet standard collateral requirements, while still applying the same discipline around documentation and cash flow.

Rates and specific terms vary by lender, by loan type and by programme, so the only reliable way to know what is on offer is to compare quotes from multiple sources before committing.

A practical pre-application checklist

Before applying, an SME owner can save months of back-and-forth by running through a short checklist:

  1. Pull together CAC documents, IDs, TIN and any required permits.
  2. Prepare twelve months of clean bank statements.
  3. Have an accountant prepare or review your financial statements.
  4. Write a one-page loan purpose statement with realistic revenue and repayment projections.
  5. Identify what collateral you can offer, and at what verified value.
  6. Check your own credit profile through a credit bureau and fix obvious errors.
  7. Compare at least three lenders, including a commercial bank, a development finance institution and a fintech or microfinance option.

The bottom line

Loan readiness in Nigeria is less about hitting a magic revenue number and more about being organised, transparent and credible. Lenders are not looking for perfect businesses; they are looking for businesses that understand their own numbers, can explain their weaknesses honestly, and have the cash flow discipline to repay what they borrow. SMEs that invest time in getting those four buckets in order will find that loan applications become faster, conversations with lenders become more productive, and approval odds improve significantly.

FAQ

What documents do Nigerian lenders ask for when reviewing an SME loan application?

Most lenders will ask for CAC registration documents, a valid means of identification, business permits where applicable, recent bank statements (usually 6 to 12 months), financial statements, and evidence of tax payments such as a TIN certificate.

Do Nigerian lenders require collateral for SME loans?

Many do, especially commercial banks, but not all. Development finance institutions and some fintech lenders may accept lighter collateral or none at all, depending on the loan size, the borrower's track record, and the specific programme.

How long does it take to get an SME loan approved in Nigeria?

Timelines vary widely. Commercial banks may take a few weeks once documents are complete, while development finance programmes can take longer because of additional review stages. Submitting clean, complete documentation is the single biggest factor in speeding things up.

Can a newly registered business in Nigeria qualify for an SME loan?

It is harder but not impossible. Lenders typically want to see at least 12 to 24 months of operating history, audited or well-kept records, and a clear business plan showing how the loan will be used and repaid.

What is the single biggest reason SME loan applications get rejected in Nigeria?

Inconsistent or missing financial records is the most common reason. Lenders cannot judge repayment ability without clean books, and applications with gaps, unreconciled statements or no tax history are usually declined or sent back for rework.

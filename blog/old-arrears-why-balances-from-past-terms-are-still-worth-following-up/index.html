Old arrears: why balances from past terms are still worth following up

Many organisations keep ledger entries for amounts that were due months or even years ago. At first glance, these old arrears look like dead weight—money that is unlikely to be collected and therefore best ignored. In practice, however, pursuing these balances can deliver tangible benefits for cash flow, risk management and regulatory compliance. Understanding why they matter and how to approach them responsibly helps businesses turn a potential loss into a recovered asset.

Why old arrears retain value

An arrear does not automatically become uncollectible simply because the original payment date has passed. The underlying obligation remains unless it has been formally discharged through settlement, waiver, or legal action such as bankruptcy discharge. As long as the debtor is still identifiable and the creditor can demonstrate a lawful basis for processing the debtor’s personal data, the debt retains economic value.

Recovering even a fraction of an old balance improves working capital. The recovered funds can be reinvested in operations, used to meet short‑term liabilities, or set aside as a buffer against future defaults. Moreover, each successful collection reduces the overall bad‑debt expense reported in financial statements, which can improve key ratios that lenders and investors monitor.

From a risk perspective, leaving old arrears on the books without review can mask deteriorating credit quality. Regular follow‑up forces the organisation to reassess the debtor’s current ability to pay, uncover any changes in financial circumstances, and decide whether to pursue, restructure, or reserve against the amount. This proactive stance prevents surprises when auditors or regulators examine the loan portfolio.

Legal and regulatory considerations

Any organisation that keeps records of who owes them money is processing personal data. In Nigeria, the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023 applies to schools, estates, businesses and lenders alike. The NDPA requires a lawful basis for processing—such as the necessity to perform a contract or the pursuit of a legitimate interest—data minimisation (collecting only what is needed for the collection purpose), and appropriate security safeguards to protect the data from unauthorised access or disclosure. The Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC) oversees compliance and can impose penalties for breaches.

When pursuing old arrears, creditors must ensure that their collection activities do not violate consumer‑protection rules. The Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC) has acted against digital lenders that engaged in shaming, harassment or contacting third parties about a borrower’s debt. Such conduct is deemed unfair and can lead to sanctions. Therefore, collection efforts should be conducted through transparent, respectful communication that focuses on the debt itself rather than on pressuring the debtor’s social network.

For lenders that participate in the banking system, the Central Bank of Nigeria’s Global Standing Instruction (GSI) policy offers a mechanism to recover non‑performing loan balances. After due process and notice, a participating bank can instruct other linked accounts held by the same debtor across the banking system to be debited via the NIBSS infrastructure. This tool can be especially useful for old arrears where the debtor maintains multiple accounts, but it must be used only after the required legal notices have been given and the debtor has been afforded an opportunity to respond.

Practical steps for follow‑up

  1. Data review and validation – Begin by confirming that the debtor’s contact information is current and that the amount recorded matches the original agreement, including any applicable fees or interest that were contractually allowed. Ensure that any personal data retained is necessary for the collection purpose and stored securely.
  2. Statute‑of‑limitations check – Verify whether any limitation period applies under Nigerian law. If the period has expired, the debt may no longer be enforceable through court action, though informal recovery attempts may still be permissible if they comply with data‑protection and consumer‑protection rules.
  3. Communication strategy – Draft a clear, concise notice that outlines the outstanding amount, the basis for the claim, and the preferred payment methods. Avoid language that could be construed as threatening, harassing, or that involves disclosing the debt to unrelated parties.
  4. Payment options – Offer flexible arrangements such as instalment plans, partial settlements, or, where appropriate, restructuring of the original terms. Demonstrating willingness to work with the debtor often improves recovery rates without resorting to aggressive tactics.
  5. Escalation pathways – If initial outreach fails, consider engaging a licensed debt‑collection agency that adheres to the NDPA and FCCPC guidelines, or, for bank‑linked loans, initiating the GSI process after fulfilling notice requirements.
  6. Documentation – Keep a record of all contacts, offers made, and payments received. This documentation supports compliance audits and provides evidence should the matter proceed to legal proceedings.

Balancing persistence with consumer protection

Persistence is valuable, but it must be exercised within the boundaries set by Nigerian law. Overly aggressive tactics—such as repeated calls at unreasonable hours, publishing the debtor’s name in public forums, or contacting friends and family about the debt—can trigger FCCPC enforcement actions and damage the organisation’s reputation. A compliant approach protects both the creditor’s right to recover money and the debtor’s right to privacy and dignity.

When a debtor demonstrates genuine financial hardship, consider whether a goodwill adjustment or a partial write‑off is more cost‑effective than continued pursuit. Such decisions should be based on a realistic assessment of recovery prospects and documented in accordance with internal policies and accounting standards.

When to write off or restructure

Not every old arrear will be recovered, and organisations must know when to shift focus. Indicators that a write‑off may be appropriate include: - The debtor is insolvent or has entered formal bankruptcy proceedings. - The cost of further collection (staff time, legal fees, agency commissions) exceeds the expected recovery. - The debt is outside any enforceable limitation period and no voluntary payment is forthcoming. - Repeated attempts to contact the debtor have failed, and there is no reasonable expectation of locating assets.

In these cases, the organisation should record the loss in line with applicable accounting standards, update provisions for bad debt, and ensure that the decision is justified and transparent. Restructuring, on the other hand, is suitable when the debtor shows willingness to pay but needs altered terms—such as extended tenure or reduced instalments—to make repayment feasible.

Conclusion

Old arrears are not merely historical entries; they represent potential cash that can strengthen an organisation’s financial position when managed correctly. By validating the debt, respecting data‑protection and consumer‑protection rules, employing measured communication strategies, and knowing when to pursue, restructure, or write off, businesses can turn dormant balances into recovered resources while staying compliant with Nigerian regulations. A disciplined, lawful approach to follow‑up protects both the creditor’s interests and the rights of the debtor, contributing to a healthier credit ecosystem overall.

FAQ

What makes an old arrear still collectible?

An arrear remains collectible as long as the underlying obligation has not been legally discharged, the debtor is still identifiable, and the creditor can demonstrate a lawful basis for processing the debtor’s personal data under the Nigeria Data Protection Act.

How does the Nigeria Data Protection Act affect collection efforts on old debts?

The NDPA requires organisations to have a lawful basis (such as contractual necessity or legitimate interest) for processing personal data, to collect only the data needed for the collection purpose, and to implement appropriate security safeguards when handling debtor information.

What role does the CBN’s Global Standing Instruction play in recovering old loan balances?

Where a borrower has defaulted on a participating bank loan, the GSI framework allows that bank, after due process and notice, to instruct other linked accounts held by the same borrower across the banking system to be debited via NIBSS to recover the outstanding balance.

Are there limits on how aggressively a creditor can pursue old arrears?

Yes. The FCCPC has taken action against practices that shame, harass or contact third parties about a debtor’s obligation. Collection must be conducted fairly, without deception or undue pressure, and must respect the debtor’s right to privacy.

When should a business consider writing off an old arrear instead of continuing collection?

Write‑off may be appropriate when the cost of further pursuit exceeds the likely recovery, when the debt is statutorily time‑barred, or when the debtor’s insolvency makes recovery impracticable, provided the decision is documented and compliant with accounting and tax rules.

Collecting Arrears and the Nigeria Data Protection Act: What Organisations Must Get Right

Organisations that collect arrears must have a lawful basis for processing personal data, limit data to what is necessary, and keep it secure under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023. They must also avoid practices that the FCCPC considers harassing or unfair. This article outlines the core requirements and practical steps to stay compliant while pursuing debt recovery.

Lawful Basis for Processing Personal Data

The NDPA requires a valid legal ground before any personal data is handled. For arrears collection, organisations typically rely on one of the following bases: - Contractual necessity: processing is needed to fulfil the loan agreement. - Legal obligation: a law or regulation mandates the collection of certain data. - Legitimate interests: the organisation has a genuine interest in recovering debt, provided it does not override the borrower’s rights. - Consent: explicit, informed agreement from the borrower (less common in arrears contexts).

Identifying and documenting the chosen basis is the first step in demonstrating compliance.

Data Minimisation and Purpose Limitation

Collect only the data that is directly needed for the debt recovery process. Typical data points include the borrower’s name, contact information, loan account number, outstanding amount, and repayment history. Avoid gathering unrelated details such as social media profiles, extensive family data, or information about third parties unless there is a clear, justified reason.

Purpose limitation means using the data solely for the stated objective—recovering the arrears—and not repurposing it for marketing, profiling, or other activities without a fresh lawful basis.

Security Safeguards for Debtor Information

The NDPA obliges organisations to protect personal data against accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure, or access. Recommended measures include: - Encrypting stored debtor records and data in transit. - Implementing role‑based access controls so only authorised staff can view sensitive information. - Conducting regular security assessments and vulnerability scans. - Maintaining an incident‑response plan to address data breaches promptly. - Training employees on data protection principles and secure handling practices.

These safeguards help ensure that debtor information remains confidential and integral throughout the collection cycle.

Avoiding Harassment and Unfair Practices

The FCCPC has highlighted that certain debt‑collection tactics can be deemed unfair or harassing. Organisations should steer clear of: - Contacting individuals listed in a borrower’s phone book or social networks without consent. - Publishing or threatening to publish a borrower’s name or debt details in a public forum to shame them. - Using abusive language, repeated calls at unreasonable hours, or threats of violence. - Misrepresenting the legal consequences of non‑payment.

Instead, adopt transparent, respectful communication: provide clear statements of the outstanding amount, explain repayment options, and give borrowers reasonable time to respond. Keeping records of all interactions can also demonstrate good‑faith efforts if disputes arise.

Frequently Asked Questions

What lawful basis can organisations rely on when processing borrower data for arrears collection? Under the NDPA, organisations must identify a lawful basis such as consent, contractual necessity, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, or legitimate interests before processing any personal data related to arrears.

How does data minimisation apply to arrears collection records? Data minimisation means collecting only the personal data that is strictly needed for the debt recovery purpose, such as contact details and loan account information, and avoiding excessive or irrelevant data.

What security measures are required under the NDPA for debtor information? Organisations must implement appropriate technical and organisational safeguards—like encryption, access controls, and regular security assessments—to protect debtor data from unauthorised access, loss, or disclosure.

What practices have the FCCPC flagged as unfair or harassing in debt collection? The FCCPC has acted against tactics such as shaming borrowers, contacting individuals in a borrower’s phone contacts, or using aggressive communication that creates undue pressure or distress.
